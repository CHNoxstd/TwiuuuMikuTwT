CHAPTER 01 / PHYSICAL SYSTEM

A changing ocean climate

Chapter summary. The ocean is an active part of the climate system: it stores and redistributes heat and carbon, influences weather and climate variability, and responds to human-caused greenhouse-gas emissions over multiple time scales.

1.1 Heat uptake and circulation

More than 90 percent of the excess heat accumulated in the Earth system due to human influence has entered the ocean. This large heat capacity slows the rate of atmospheric warming at the surface, but it does not remove the energy imbalance. Ocean warming is uneven across depth and region; currents, winds, mixing, and air–sea exchange shape where and when heat accumulates. Changes in ocean heat content therefore inform climate monitoring, while local marine heat exposure also depends on seasonal conditions and regional circulation.

Ocean circulation connects distant regions by transporting heat, freshwater, nutrients, and carbon. Circulation varies naturally and can change as the ocean warms and receives additional freshwater. A single index cannot capture all circulation processes, and short observational records make attribution and trend detection difficult in some regions. Monitoring should combine sustained measurements with physical understanding and communicate the time scales represented.

1.2 Sea level and coastal change

Global mean sea level rises as seawater expands when it warms and as land-based glaciers and ice sheets lose mass. The global mean is a useful indicator, not a local water-level forecast. Relative sea level at a coast also reflects land motion, gravitational and rotational effects of ice loss, ocean circulation, tides, and atmospheric pressure. These factors can amplify or offset the global signal at a particular site.

Higher baseline sea levels increase the reach of some coastal floods, but the consequences depend on shoreline shape, waves, storm tracks, sediment movement, ecosystems, and built infrastructure. Planning based only on a historical tide record may underestimate future conditions; projections should be presented as ranges and paired with explicit assumptions, local observations, and regular review.

1.3 Carbon chemistry and oxygen

The ocean absorbs a substantial share of anthropogenic carbon dioxide. This uptake changes seawater carbonate chemistry and lowers average pH, a process known as ocean acidification. Acidification is a change in chemical conditions, not a claim that seawater has become acidic in the everyday sense. Impacts vary by species, life stage, habitat, and exposure to other stressors such as warming, low oxygen, and pollution.

Deoxygenation has multiple causes, including warming-related changes in oxygen solubility and ventilation, as well as local nutrient inputs that can increase oxygen consumption. Conditions differ by depth and basin, and biological responses are not uniform. Long-term, quality-controlled observations are needed to distinguish climate trends from local pressures and natural variability.

1.4 Evidence, uncertainty, and use

Confidence is strongest where multiple independent lines of evidence agree: physical theory, instrumental records, paleoclimate information, and model projections. Uncertainty remains about regional detail, internal variability, and some process feedbacks. Communicating a range is not evidence that nothing is known; it shows what observations and methods can support and what decisions must remain robust across plausible futures.
