CHAPTER 03 / RISK

Risk, exposure & vulnerability

Chapter summary. Climate-related risk emerges through interactions among hazards, exposure, and vulnerability. These elements are shaped by both physical change and social, economic, and institutional conditions.

3.1 From hazard to consequence

Sea-level rise, coastal flooding, erosion, marine heat, and changing ecosystems are hazards or stressors, not impacts in isolation. Consequences depend on what is exposed and on the sensitivity and adaptive capacity of people, infrastructure, livelihoods, and ecological systems. A coastal flood can disrupt transport or health services well beyond the flooded area; ecosystem decline may affect food access and cultural practice as well as species abundance.

Risk is dynamic. Development patterns, land use, protective infrastructure, public services, and policy decisions change exposure and vulnerability over time. Historical choices can leave some groups with less secure housing, fewer resources, or less influence over coastal planning. Treating present-day vulnerability as a natural feature of a place obscures these drivers and the possibility of change.

3.2 Uneven impacts and justice

People within the same region do not experience risk equally. Income, age, health, disability, occupation, migration status, cultural ties, access to information, and legal rights can shape both exposure and recovery. The distribution of benefits and burdens also matters: a protective project may reduce risk for one area while increasing erosion, access restrictions, or displacement elsewhere.

Equity is not achieved simply by adding a social indicator to a map. Assessment processes should ask who defines the problem, whose data and knowledge count, who participates in decisions, and who can appeal or revise them. Community knowledge can identify seasonal use, past events, informal protection, and overlooked impacts that standard datasets do not capture.

3.3 Evidence, scenarios, and uncertainty

Risk assessments combine observations, models, scenarios, and expert or local knowledge. Each source answers different questions and has limitations. Scenarios describe plausible pathways under stated assumptions; they are not predictions with guaranteed probabilities. Model resolution, data gaps, changing coastlines, and compounding events can affect results. A clear assessment states its geographic boundary, time horizon, assumptions, and confidence.

Maps and indices can help organize information, but they can create false precision if inputs, weighting, or uncertainty are hidden. Results should be checked against local experience and updated as conditions and decisions change. Where data are limited, the appropriate response is to communicate the gap and support better observation—not to treat absence of evidence as evidence of safety.

3.4 Compound and cascading events

Multiple events can coincide or interact: high tide with storm surge, heavy rainfall with coastal flooding, heat with drought, or infrastructure failure with disrupted emergency services. Their combined effects may cascade through energy, water, transport, health, and food systems. Planning that addresses each hazard in isolation can miss dependencies and bottlenecks.

Preparedness benefits from locally grounded early warning, accessible communication, continuity planning, and coordination across agencies and neighboring communities. These measures reduce some consequences but cannot remove the underlying hazard or substitute for long-term emissions reduction and equitable land-use choices.
