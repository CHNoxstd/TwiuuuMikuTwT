CHAPTER 04 / RESPONSES

Adaptation, governance & equity

Chapter summary. Adaptation is an ongoing process of learning and decision-making under changing conditions. Effective responses address current needs while avoiding the transfer of risk to other people, places, or generations.

4.1 A portfolio of responses

Coastal adaptation can include early-warning systems, improved public services, building and infrastructure standards, ecosystem protection and restoration, land-use planning, financial instruments, and—in some circumstances—planned relocation. These approaches can be combined and sequenced. Their suitability depends on local exposure, ecological dynamics, governance, resources, and the priorities of affected communities.

Hard infrastructure may provide important protection but can have ecological, financial, and distributional consequences and may create a false sense of permanent safety. Nature-based measures can provide multiple benefits when ecological conditions allow, but their performance is context-dependent and they require space, stewardship, and monitoring. Neither approach should be selected from a generic menu without assessing alternatives and trade-offs.

4.2 Maladaptation and trade-offs

Maladaptation occurs when an action increases risk, vulnerability, or inequality for some people or systems, now or in the future. Examples can include protection that encourages risky development behind a barrier, restoration that restricts customary access, or relocation without secure housing, livelihoods, and participation. These outcomes are not inevitable, but avoiding them requires attention to rights, distribution, and long-term feedbacks.

Evaluation should look beyond a project's immediate outputs. Relevant questions include who benefits and who pays, whether residual risk is communicated, what happens under more severe conditions, and whether the action can be revised or reversed. Transparent monitoring and independent review help reveal unintended effects early enough to respond.

4.3 Governance and meaningful participation

Coastal decisions involve different levels of government, community organizations, Indigenous Peoples, researchers, businesses, and civil society. Clear responsibilities, access to information, inclusive processes, and accountable institutions support coherent action. Participation is meaningful when people can influence goals and choices—not simply provide information after a decision has already been made.

Local and Indigenous knowledge can contribute long records of environmental change, place-based indicators, and approaches to stewardship. Such knowledge must be included with consent, appropriate attribution, and respect for governance and data rights. Scientific assessment and lived knowledge can inform one another without being treated as interchangeable or stripped from their context.

4.4 Adaptation alongside mitigation

Adaptation addresses impacts that are occurring or expected; mitigation reduces the greenhouse-gas emissions that drive climate change and strengthens removals. Both are necessary. Coastal ecosystems may contribute to adaptation and carbon storage, but their capacity is limited and vulnerable to disturbance. Protecting or restoring them cannot compensate for ongoing fossil-fuel emissions or justify delaying rapid, sustained emissions reductions.

Durable adaptation requires predictable funding, institutional capacity, social protection, and continued learning. Plans should identify who is responsible, how success and harm will be measured, when actions will be reviewed, and how communities can seek remedy. Flexible pathways can support timely decisions while preserving options as evidence and conditions evolve.
