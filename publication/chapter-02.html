CHAPTER 02 / ECOSYSTEMS

Coastal ecosystems & biodiversity

Chapter summary. Coastal and marine ecosystems are diverse, dynamic, and connected to the landscapes and communities around them. Their condition reflects interacting climate pressures and local human activities.

2.1 A mosaic of habitats

Coral reefs, mangroves, tidal marshes, seagrass meadows, kelp forests, estuaries, rocky shores, and deep-sea habitats differ in species composition, physical setting, and ecological function. Each supports particular food webs and life cycles. Many habitats are linked: a fish may use nursery, feeding, and spawning grounds in different places or at different life stages. Protecting a patch without considering connectivity can therefore leave important ecological relationships exposed.

Habitat labels can conceal substantial variation within a type. A mangrove forest on a sediment-rich delta differs from one on a carbonate island; a reef's structure and recovery potential depend on local ecology, water quality, and disturbance history. Monitoring should describe the specific place, measurement methods, and temporal coverage rather than assuming a habitat category predicts a uniform outcome.

2.2 Ecological functions and limits

Vegetated coastal ecosystems can store carbon in biomass and sediments, and some reefs, wetlands, and other habitats can reduce wave energy under particular conditions. These functions are not interchangeable, unlimited, or guaranteed. Their magnitude depends on ecosystem extent and health, local hydrodynamics, sea-level change, sediment supply, and management. Carbon storage estimates, for example, require transparent boundaries and long-term accounting for disturbance and permanence.

Healthy ecosystems have intrinsic value and support biodiversity, food systems, cultural practice, and livelihoods. Their conservation is important whether or not a specific protective or carbon benefit can be quantified. Ecosystem services should complement, not replace, recognition of rights, values, and ecological thresholds.

2.3 Cumulative pressures and resilience

Marine heatwaves, warming, acidification, low oxygen, pollution, extraction, coastal construction, and habitat fragmentation can occur together. The combined effect may be greater—or sometimes different—than the sum of individual pressures. A one-time survey may miss cumulative exposure, delayed effects, or seasonal shifts. Repeated observations, reference conditions, and locally meaningful indicators improve interpretation.

Resilience is not an immutable property that can be represented by one universal score. It describes the capacity of an ecological or social-ecological system to absorb disturbance, reorganize, or transform while retaining valued functions. Which functions matter, who benefits, and what change is acceptable are questions that require open discussion, not only ecological measurement.

2.4 Conservation and restoration

Effective conservation can include safeguarding intact habitat, reducing pollution and extraction pressures, maintaining ecological connectivity, and supporting locally led governance. Restoration is most credible when it addresses the causes of degradation and is guided by reference ecosystems, long-term monitoring, and realistic ecological limits. Planting or construction targets alone do not demonstrate durable recovery.

Climate change may shift suitable habitat and increase disturbance. Adaptive management can respond to new evidence, but it must be paired with sustained funding, transparent reporting, and decision-making that recognizes local rights and knowledge. No single ecosystem intervention can compensate for continued greenhouse-gas emissions or eliminate all coastal risk.
